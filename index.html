The Witness Remains

This archive preserves a documentary record assembled over many years. It contains correspondence, official records, statements, legal material, medical records, evidence references, published material, personal testimony and other documents.

The archive distinguishes between primary records, personal testimony, allegations, interpretation, analysis and material published by the author. Inclusion of a document does not by itself establish the truth of every proposition contained within it.

The purpose of preservation is to allow the record to remain available for examination, comparison and verification against the underlying source documents.

Archive Principle

The record remains available for scrutiny. Documents are retained so that dates, names, correspondence, institutional responses and changes in position can be examined in their original documentary context.

Where the archive records an allegation, it is treated as an allegation unless independently established by the underlying evidence. Where the archive reproduces an official statement, it is identified as an official statement rather than treated automatically as proof of the proposition asserted.

METHODOLOGY

Forensic Documentary Method

The archive should be examined using a source-first method.

  1. Identify the original document.
  2. Record the date and document type.
  3. Identify the sender, recipient and institution where available.
  4. Record the exact proposition contained in the document.
  5. Separate fact, allegation, interpretation and conclusion.
  6. Locate preceding and subsequent correspondence.
  7. Compare the document against independent records where available.
  8. Record uncertainty rather than filling gaps with inference.

Chronological Reconstruction

Chronology is particularly important where an allegation concerns a sequence of administrative decisions. The existence of a later event does not by itself prove that an earlier actor intended that later result.

Cross-Agency Comparison

Where the same matter appears in records from different agencies, the records can be compared for consistency, differences in terminology, changes in factual description and differences in procedural treatment.

Authenticity and Provenance

The preferred record is the original document or the closest available copy whose provenance can be established. Screenshots, summaries and later transcriptions should be identified as such.

VERIFICATION

Verification Standard

A proposition is strongest where it can be checked directly against an identifiable primary source.

Where independent verification is unavailable, the archive should state that limitation rather than presenting the proposition as established fact.

Verification Categories

Status Meaning
Primary-source verified The proposition can be checked directly against the identified source record.
Corroborated More than one independent or materially separate record supports the proposition.
Reported The proposition is recorded as someone’s account or allegation.
Unresolved The available records do not permit a definitive conclusion.
Interpretive The proposition represents analysis rather than a direct documentary fact.

OPEN CHALLENGE

Invitation to Examine the Record

The archive invites examination of the underlying documentary material rather than reliance upon the author's conclusions alone.

A credible challenge should identify the specific document, proposition, date or interpretation being challenged and provide the evidence supporting the alternative account.

Likewise, a documentary proposition should not be treated as established merely because it appears repeatedly in later summaries. Repetition is not a substitute for primary-source verification.

Questions for Review

Connections that represent interpretation rather than direct documentary evidence should be labelled as such.

SOURCE AUTHENTICATION

Authentication concerns whether a document is what it purports to be.

Useful indicators include original file provenance, metadata, sender information, digital signatures where present, registry information, email headers, document numbering and corroborating records.

A screenshot or transcription should not automatically be treated as equivalent to the original source.

DOCUMENT VERSIONING

Where a document exists in multiple versions, the archive should preserve each version rather than silently replacing an earlier copy.

The version history should identify the creation or receipt date where known and the relationship between the versions.

ATTACHMENT INTEGRITY

Email attachments form part of the documentary context of the email that transmitted them.

Where possible, attachments should be preserved with the associated message and should retain their original filename.

EMAIL PROVENANCE

Email provenance is strengthened by retaining sender, recipient, date, subject, message identifier and attachment information.

Where only a copied body is available, the archive should identify it as a copy or transcription rather than implying that complete email-header information has been preserved.

TRANSCRIPTION METHOD

Transcriptions should preserve the substantive wording of the source while correcting only presentation artefacts introduced by extraction where necessary for readability.

Any editorial intervention should be identifiable rather than silently incorporated into the source text.

PDF EXTRACTION

PDF text extraction can introduce artefacts including broken words, duplicated headers, missing characters, misplaced page numbers and altered paragraph boundaries.

Where extracted text appears inconsistent with the source document, the page image or original PDF should control.

PAGE MARKER INTEGRITY

Standalone extraction markers such as “PAGE 7” or “PAGE 8” are presentation metadata when they are generated by a PDF extraction process.

They should not be allowed to interrupt substantive sentences or paragraphs in the public-facing archive.

Removing such presentation markers does not remove the underlying substantive text from the source.

ARCHIVE CONTEXT

Every significant proposition should, where practical, be accompanied by sufficient context to allow a reader to understand what document it came from and what role that document played.

Context includes date, source, recipient, preceding communication and subsequent response.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT

Documents created decades apart should not automatically be treated as if they were created under the same institutional or legal circumstances.

Historical context includes the governing legislation, institutional structure, terminology and procedural system applicable at the time.

INSTITUTIONAL CHANGE

Government agencies, departments and tribunals can change names, functions and jurisdiction over time.

The archive should preserve the institution's historical identity at the time the source was created while using modern cross-references where helpful.

AGENCY RESPONSIBILITY

Responsibility should be assigned at the level supported by the record.

An agency may be responsible for processing a document without being responsible for the underlying event described in the document.

DECISION CHAIN

A decision chain identifies the sequence from application or complaint through assessment, recommendation, decision, review and outcome.

The chain can reveal where information entered the process and where it changed.

INFORMATION FLOW

The archive can be analysed as an information-flow system: information is created, transmitted, received, classified, acted upon or omitted.

The documentary question is not merely whether information existed, but who had it and when.

RECORDED KNOWLEDGE

A statement appearing in an official record establishes that the statement was recorded. It does not necessarily establish that the institution independently verified the statement.

The archive therefore distinguishes recorded knowledge from verified knowledge.

INSTITUTIONAL LANGUAGE

Terminology used by agencies can affect how a matter is classified and processed.

The archive preserves significant terminology so that changes in language over time can be examined.

CLASSIFICATION EFFECTS

An administrative classification can determine which procedure, institution or review mechanism applies.

Where classification is disputed, the archive should preserve both the classification and the evidence relied upon to challenge it.

REVIEW PROCESS

Reviews provide an opportunity to compare an original decision with later consideration of the same issue.

The existence of a review does not necessarily mean that the original decision was wrong; the review record should be examined for its actual result.

COMPLAINT PROCESS

A complaint record should identify the issue raised, the institution receiving it, the response provided and any review or escalation.

The archive preserves complaint pathways so that the reader can reconstruct whether an issue was addressed, referred, closed or left unresolved.

ESCALATION

Escalation occurs where a matter is taken from an initial decision-maker to a higher administrative, complaint, tribunal or judicial pathway.

The archive records escalation as a procedural fact where supported by the relevant documents.

INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE ANALYSIS

Institutional responses can be classified according to whether they provide substantive information, request further material, refer the matter, decline jurisdiction, close the matter, or provide a determination.

The classification should be based on the wording of the response rather than the author's characterisation alone.

ARCHIVE NEUTRALITY

The archive's documentary sections aim to preserve the distinction between evidence and interpretation.

This does not prevent the author from expressing conclusions elsewhere in the publication. It ensures that the reader can identify which statements are source-derived and which are analytical.

ANALYTICAL LIMITATIONS

No retrospective archive can guarantee that every relevant document has survived or been obtained.

Missing documents, incomplete metadata, inaccessible records, duplicate files and later-created summaries can affect the analysis.

Those limitations should be stated rather than concealed.

ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE

The absence of a document from the archive does not necessarily establish that the event did not occur.

Likewise, the absence of a response in a particular collection does not necessarily establish that no response existed elsewhere.

Negative conclusions require a sufficiently complete search.

CORROBORATION

Corroboration is strongest when independent records support the same proposition without simply copying the same original assertion.

Multiple records derived from a single source should not automatically be treated as independent corroboration.

CONTRADICTION

A contradiction should be recorded precisely rather than described generally.

The archive should identify the two propositions, the documents in which they appear and whether a later source resolves the difference.

DOCUMENTARY CONFIDENCE

Documentary confidence should be proportional to the quality of the source and corroboration.

Level Description
High Direct primary source with clear provenance and independent corroboration.
Moderate Primary source available but limited corroboration or context.
Reported Reliable record of an allegation or personal account without independent confirmation.
Unresolved Conflicting or incomplete evidence prevents a firm conclusion.

DOCUMENTARY STANDARD

The standard adopted by this archive is preservation before conclusion.

No proposition should become stronger merely because it has been repeated many times. Repetition can establish that an allegation was repeatedly made; it does not independently establish the truth of the allegation.

Conversely, an adverse document should not be removed merely because it conflicts with the author's interpretation.

CONTRADICTIONS

Contradictions are preserved because they may be among the most significant parts of the documentary record.

Where two institutions provide materially different accounts of the same event, both accounts should be retained and the difference clearly identified.

UNRESOLVED QUESTIONS

The archive deliberately retains questions that cannot presently be resolved from the available evidence.

  • Who made particular decisions?
  • What information was available to the decision-maker?
  • Why was a particular jurisdiction invoked?
  • Why was a particular complaint closed or redirected?
  • What evidence was considered?
  • What evidence was not considered?
  • Were records transferred between institutions?
  • Were relevant records subsequently altered, lost or superseded?

Provenance is the chain connecting an archive item to its source and acquisition history.
